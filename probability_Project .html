CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY

Conditional probability means calculating within a restricted group. For a fair die, let A be “even” and B be “greater than 3.” Without extra information P(A) = 3/6. Given B, the only possible faces are {4,5,6}; two are even, so P(A | B) = 2/3.

The bar | reads “given.” It does not mean divide, and it does not mean B causes A. A and B can describe properties of the same result.

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), provided P(B) > 0

Dividing by P(B) makes the whole B group the new 100%. In the example, (2/6)/(3/6) = 2/3. Reversing the condition changes the reference group: P(A | B) is generally different from P(B | A).

Build a sequence of events

P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B | A), provided P(A) > 0

Imagine a probability tree. Multiply conditional probabilities along one path. Add probabilities of mutually exclusive paths that lead to the event you want. Independence is the special case where a later branch probability is unchanged by the earlier result.

Law of total probability: combine all routes

If B₁ and B₂ cannot happen together and cover all possibilities, an event A can occur through either route. Their weighted contributions add:

P(A) = P(B₁)P(A | B₁) + P(B₂)P(A | B₂)

Each displayed conditional requires its conditioning event to have positive probability. The same idea works for more than two disjoint groups. The bowl game uses it directly: choose a bowl, then a marble. Monty Hall shows why the rule used to reveal information matters.

COMBINATIONS & MATCHING

Counting helps us find how many possibilities exist without listing them individually. We use it to describe possible outcomes, select groups, arrange objects, assign roles, and form codes.

Two questions determine how we count

Does order matter? Choosing Ada and Ben for a team gives the same team as choosing Ben and Ada. But assigning Ada as chair and Ben as secretary is different from reversing their roles. Combinations count selections where order does not matter; permutations count ordered arrangements of distinct items.

Can a choice be repeated? A code may allow the same digit several times. Selecting people for a team usually allows each person only once. Repetition and other restrictions change the number of available choices.

How counting connects to probability

When an experiment has a finite number of equally likely outcomes, count all possible outcomes and those that satisfy the event you want:

P(event) = favourable outcomes / total possible outcomes

If outcomes are not equally likely, counting alone is insufficient: we must also account for their individual probabilities.

BAYESIAN INFERENCE

Bayes’ theorem updates a probability after evidence arrives. The probability before that evidence is the prior; the updated probability is the posterior. It does not require the prior to be a subjective guess: it can come from a known population rate.

Reverse the condition carefully

“How often does a defective component trigger an alarm?” is P(alarm | defect). “How likely is a defect when an alarm sounds?” is P(defect | alarm). These are different fractions with different denominators.

P(A | B) = P(B | A)P(A) / P(B), for P(B) > 0

P(B | A) describes how likely the evidence is under A. The denominator counts all ways that evidence B can occur:

P(B) = P(B | A)P(A) + P(B | Aᶜ)P(Aᶜ)

An alarm example

Suppose 10% of components are defective. The alarm sounds for 80% of defective components and 20% of good ones. In 1,000 components, expect 80 alarms from 100 defective units and 180 alarms from 900 good units.

Among the 260 alarms, 80 come from defects. P(defect | alarm) = 80/260 ≈ 30.77%, not 80%. This is the effect of the base rate: there are many more good components available to create false alarms.

Probability and odds are different

A probability p compares favourable cases with all cases. Odds compare favourable with unfavourable cases: odds = p/(1−p), for p < 1. A probability of 0.25 corresponds to odds 1:3. The calculators in this playground report probabilities.

The next activity applies the same counting logic to a hypothetical test. Its inputs are a mathematical model, not an assessment of an individual’s health.

THE EMPEROR'S DILEMMA

You have 50 white and 50 black marbles. Place all 100 into two bowls, with at least one marble in each. The Emperor first chooses a bowl with probability 1/2, then chooses a marble uniformly from that bowl. A white marble wins freedom.

Two random stages, two different probabilities

A bowl with fewer marbles is still chosen half the time. If Bowl A contains wA white and bA black marbles, then after A is selected, the white probability is wA/(wA+bA). The corresponding expression holds for Bowl B.

P(white) = ½ × wA/(wA+bA) + ½ × wB/(wB+bB)

We multiply along each route and add the two disjoint routes. This is the law of total probability. It is not the same experiment as choosing one marble uniformly from all 100 at once, which would always give 50/100.

Check a starting arrangement

With 25 white and 25 black in each bowl, the chance is ½×(25/50) + ½×(25/50) = 1/2. If A has 10 white and no black, B has 40 white and 50 black: the chance becomes ½×1 + ½×(40/90) = 13/18 ≈ 72.22%.

Your challenge

Can you do better than 72.22%? Change Bowl A; the unused marbles automatically go to B. Calculate the probability, then test individual draws or a fresh batch. Every draw uses the same arrangement; marbles are not depleted between trials.

Reveal the best arrangement and why it works

Put 1 white marble alone in A, and 49 white plus all 50 black in B. Then P(white) = ½×1 + ½×(49/99) = 74/99 ≈ 74.7475%.

To see why this is optimal, call the smaller bowl’s size m, with 1 ≤ m ≤ 50, and its white count w. The probability is ½[w/m + (50−w)/(100−m)]. For fixed m ≤ 50 this cannot decrease when w increases, so fill the smaller bowl with white marbles. The result is ½[1 + (50−m)/(100−m)], which decreases as m increases. Thus m = 1 is best. Swapping the bowl labels gives the same optimum.

BIRTHDAY PARADOX

In a room, ask whether any two people share a birthday. This is different from asking whether somebody shares your birthday: there are many more pairs when everybody can match everybody else.

State the model first

Assume 365 possible birthdays, equally likely for each person, and independent birthdays. Ignore leap day. Real birthdays have seasonal patterns and family relationships can create dependence, so this is a simplified model.

Count the opposite event

“At least one match” includes pairs, triples and many overlaps. “No match” is easier: the first birthday can be any of 365 days; the second must avoid one used day; the third must avoid two; and so on.

P(no match) = 1 × (364/365) × (363/365) × ⋯ × [(365−n+1)/365]
P(at least one match) = 1 − P(no match)

Each factor is conditional on all earlier birthdays being different. For n ≤ 365 the product gives the exact probability under the model. With 366 people a match is certain because there are more people than possible dates.

23 people

There are 23×22/2 = 253 distinct pairs. Multiplying the 23 no-match factors gives approximately 0.492703. Its complement is 0.507297, or 50.7297%.

By contrast, the chance that at least one of 22 other people shares one specified person’s birthday is 1 − (364/365)²², about 5.86%.

Try a room, then many rooms

Choose the room size. Generate one room to see the actual birthdays; matching dates are highlighted. A single room can have no match even when a match is likely. Simulate many independent rooms to compare their match frequency with the theoretical probability. Each simulation run starts fresh.

Why birthday pairs use n(n−1)/2

Choose the first person in n ways and a different second person in n−1 ways. Divide by 2 because A–B and B–A are the same pair. This gives C(n,2) = n(n−1)/2. With 23 people there are 253 pairs.

Counting pairs does not make their match events independent. Do not use 253 independent coin-like chances to calculate the exact birthday probability; use the no-match method in the next activity.

PROBABILITY BASICS

Try the experiment ↓

Roll a die before looking at it. You know which results are possible, but you do not know which one will occur. Probability describes this uncertainty. It assigns a number between 0 and 1 to an event: a larger number means the event is more likely.

From an experiment to an event

An experiment is the action, such as one die roll. An outcome is one complete result, such as 4. The sample space, written S, is the collection of all possible outcomes. For one six-sided die, S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Braces { } simply mean “the set containing these items.”

An event is a collection of outcomes that satisfy a condition. Let A mean “an even number appears.” Then A = {2, 4, 6}. The event A happens whenever the result belongs to this set. P(A), read “the probability of A,” is one number describing how likely that event is.

When is counting enough?

A fair die makes all six faces equally likely. Three of them satisfy A, so P(A) = 3/6 = 0.5 = 50%. A 50% chance means about half of many repetitions, not a promise of exactly half in a small batch.

For equally likely outcomes: P(A) = number in A / number in S

This counting shortcut needs equally likely outcomes. A loaded die may favour some faces. Then add the probabilities of the faces in A: P(A) = P(2) + P(4) + P(6). Counting three faces alone would not determine the answer.

NotationMeaning for this die
P(S) = 1Some face in the sample space must occur.
∅The empty set: an event containing no outcomes. P(∅) = 0.
AᶜThe complement of A: all outcomes outside A. Here {1, 3, 5}.

A model and an experiment answer different questions

Theoretical probability comes from an assumed model, such as a fair die. Experimental relative frequency is the number of observed successes divided by the number of trials. If 47 of 100 rolls are even, the observed frequency is 47/100 = 0.47. That does not change the fair-die model from 0.50 to 0.47; it is one random sample.

EVENTS & PROBABILITY RULES

Probability rules describe how events fit together. Keep one example throughout: roll a fair die, let A = {2, 4, 6} mean “even,” and B = {4, 5, 6} mean “greater than 3.”

“And” is the overlap

A ∩ B, read “A intersection B,” contains results satisfying both conditions. Here A ∩ B = {4, 6}, so P(A ∩ B) = 2/6. This is also called a joint probability.

“Or” includes either event, including both

A ∪ B, read “A union B,” contains {2, 4, 5, 6}. Adding 3/6 + 3/6 would count both 4 and 6 twice. Subtract the overlap once.

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
Here: 3/6 + 3/6 − 2/6 = 4/6

Mutually exclusive is different from independent

Mutually exclusive events cannot both happen on the same trial. “Roll 1” and “roll 2” have no overlap. Their union has probability 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3.

Independent events do not change each other’s probability. Independence is a property of the model, not a synonym for “different events.” In the A and B example, P(A ∩ B) = 1/3 but P(A)P(B) = 1/4, so A and B are not independent.

Independence test: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)

Two mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities cannot be independent: if one happens, the other becomes impossible. Events of probability zero are an exception to that statement.

Why multiplying is not always “and”

The general rule is P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B | A), when P(A) > 0. The vertical bar means “given.” It asks for the chance of B after restricting attention to A. Only for independent events can P(B | A) be replaced by P(B).

Two draws from a bag

A bag has 2 red balls and 1 blue. Draw two without replacement. The chance of red on both draws is (2/3) × (1/2) = 1/3. After the first red, only 1 of the 2 remaining balls is red. If you replace and remix the first ball, the probability becomes (2/3) × (2/3) = 4/9.

“At least one” often becomes easier through “none”

For two independent fair flips, “at least one head” includes HH, HT and TH. Its complement contains only TT.

P(at least one H) = 1 − P(no H) = 1 − (1/2)² = 3/4

“Exactly one head” excludes HH, so its probability is 2/4. Read words such as exactly, at least, at most carefully: they define different events.

COIN FLIPS — INDEPENDENCE

Try the experiment ↓

Two assumptions do different jobs. Fair means each flip has probability 1/2 of heads. Independent means learning earlier results does not change the next flip’s probabilities. A biased coin can still have independent flips; a 50–50 mechanism can still produce dependent results.

Does a streak change the next flip?

Under the independent fair-coin model, five heads give no new information about the next result. Heads and tails are each still 50%. Thinking that tails must now compensate for earlier heads is the gambler’s fallacy.

P(next H | five previous H) = 1/2

Before the sequence and after the sequence are different questions

Before flipping six times, the probability of HHHHHH is (1/2)⁶ = 1/64. Once five heads have already occurred, those results are known. Only the sixth flip remains uncertain, so its chance of heads is 1/2, not 1/64.

Any particular six-flip pattern, including HTHTTH, also has probability 1/64. But “three heads in any order” includes 20 different patterns, giving 20/64. One pattern and a collection of patterns are different events.

Random does not mean perfectly alternating

Streaks are a normal feature of independent trials. In a longer sequence there are more opportunities for a streak to appear. A run of heads alone does not show that the coin is unfair or dependent.

The proportion of heads tends toward 1/2 over many independent flips. It need not move closer after every flip, and the counts of heads and tails do not have to become equal.

DICE & LAW OF LARGE NUMBERS

Try the experiment ↓

Let X be the number shown by one fair die. The capital letter names a quantity whose value is uncertain before the roll. Once a particular roll gives 4, its observed value is x = 4. We use lower-case x for a particular value.

Expected value is a weighted average

E[X], read “the expected value of X,” weights each possible value by its probability and adds the results. The symbol Σ means “add over all possible values.”

E[X] = Σ x P(X=x)
For a fair die: 1×(1/6) + 2×(1/6) + ⋯ + 6×(1/6) = 3.5

3.5 is not a face you can roll, and it is not the most likely face. It is the average approached over many rolls. For a biased die, use its actual face probabilities rather than dividing the sum by six.

Expected value does not mean a guaranteed payoff

A game pays $6 for rolling a six and $0 otherwise. The expected payout is 6×(1/6) + 0×(5/6) = $1. If entry costs $2, expected net gain is $1 − $2 = −$1 per play. One play can still win $4 net.

Sample mean and the Law of Large Numbers

A sample is the rolls you actually collect. For n rolls with observed values x₁, …, xₙ, their average is the sample mean x̄ = (x₁ + ⋯ + xₙ)/n. Unlike the fixed model mean 3.5, this average changes from sample to sample.

For independent rolls with the same distribution, the Law of Large Numbers (LLN) says the sample mean converges to E[X] as the sample grows. More precisely, the probability of an error larger than any chosen positive tolerance tends to zero. This does not mean every new roll improves the average or that a fixed sample size guarantees a particular accuracy.

Frequency follows the same idea

Record 1 when a six appears and 0 otherwise. The average of these indicators equals the fraction of sixes. Its expected value is 1/6, so the relative frequency converges to 1/6 under the same model.

Toss two independent fair coins. The four equally likely outcomes are HH, HT, TH and TT. Suppose the only thing you want to record is how many heads appeared. This turns each full outcome into a number.

1. Random variable

Call the number of heads X. X is a random variable: its rule is fixed, but its value depends on the random outcome. Capital X names the variable; lower-case x names a particular possible or observed value. P(X = 1) means “the probability that the number of heads equals one.”

X has possible values {0,1,2}, called its support. These values are not equally likely: two different outcomes give X = 1. A distribution keeps track of those probabilities.

2. Prpbabaility Mass Function (PMF)

A discrete variable takes values from a finite or countably infinite list, such as 0,1,2,… . Its probability mass function (PMF) is p(x) = P(X = x). Here p(0) = 1/4, p(1) = 1/2 and p(2) = 1/4. The lower-case p(x) means a function evaluated at x; it is not a new random variable.

Every p(x) ≥ 0, and Σ p(x) = 1
P(X belongs to an event) = add its PMF values

The symbol Σ means “sum.” For example, P(X ≥ 1) = p(1) + p(2) = 3/4. On a PMF graph, each bar’s height is its probability. Bar width is only a drawing choice; you do not multiply by width.

3. Cumulative distribution function (CDF)”

The cumulative distribution function (CDF), F(x) = P(X ≤ x), adds everything to the left including x. It works for every random variable, discrete or continuous.

x rangeF(x) for the two-coin example

0 ≤ x < 11/4

Thus F(1.5) = 3/4 even though X can never equal 1.5. There is no new probability between 1 and 2, so the CDF stays flat. At a possible value k, the upward jump equals P(X = k). The value at the jump includes that new probability.

For an integer-valued X:
P(X ≤ k) = F(k)
P(X < k) = F(k−1)
P(X ≥ k) = 1 − F(k−1)
P(X > k) = 1 − F(k)

A CDF never decreases, stays between 0 and 1, and approaches 0 at the far left and 1 at the far right. For integers a ≤ b, P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a−1).

4. Continuous probability

Now measure a waiting time T. A continuous model allows values anywhere in an interval, rather than on a countable list. For the continuous models taught here, probability is described by a probability density function (PDF), f(t). Its height is density; area is probability.

If a wait is uniformly distributed from 0 to 10 minutes, its density is 1/10 per minute. The chance of waiting between 2 and 5 minutes is the rectangular area (5−2)×(1/10) = 0.3.

P(a ≤ T ≤ b) = area under f(t) from a to b = F(b) − F(a)

The integral notation ∫ab f(t) dt means this area. You can understand the idea without doing integration: the total area is 1, and an interval gets its share of that area.

A single point has zero width, so P(T = t) = 0 in these continuous models. That does not mean the observed value is forbidden. A recorded “3.0 minutes,” rounded to the nearest tenth, represents an interval approximately from 2.95 to 3.05 minutes, which can have positive probability. Including or excluding a single endpoint does not change a continuous interval probability.

A density may exceed 1. A uniform value on [0,0.2] has density 5, but total area 0.2×5 = 1. Density has reciprocal measurement units; it is not a percentage.

5. Mean, expected value, variance and standard deviation

A probability distribution tells us which values can occur and how likely they are. We often want to summarize it by answering two questions: Where is its centre? and How widely are its values spread around that centre?

Mean: the average of the observations you collected

Suppose you play a game three times and receive 0, 20 and 20 points. To find the mean, add the scores and divide by the number of plays.

Mean of these observations = (0 + 20 + 20) / 3
≈ 13.33 points

This is a sample mean: it summarizes the particular results you observed. Another set of three plays could produce a different average.

Expected value: the mean of the probability distribution

Now suppose we know the rules of the game: each play gives either 0 points or 20 points, with probability 1/2 each. Let X represent the points from one play.

To find the distribution's mean, multiply each possible value by its probability, then add the contributions. This is a probability-weighted average. More likely values receive more weight.

Possible points Probability Value × probability
0 1/2 0 × 1/2 = 0
20 1/2 20 × 1/2 = 10

This distribution mean is called the expected value. We write it as E[X], or μ (the Greek letter “mu”). These are two names for the same quantity.

μ = E[X] = 0 × (1/2) + 20 × (1/2) = 10 points

Over many independent plays under these same rules, roughly half the results will be 0 and half will be 20. Their average tends toward 10 points as the number of plays grows. A finite run can have a different average, and each extra play does not necessarily bring it closer to 10.

Expected value describes an average across repeated plays, not the result of one play. Here, you can never receive exactly 10 points in a single play, even though 10 is the expected value.

The probability weights matter. If the chance of 0 were 1/4 and the chance of 20 were 3/4, the expected value would instead be 0 × 1/4 + 20 × 3/4 = 15 points.

In the general discrete formula below, x represents a possible value, P(X = x) is its probability, and Σ means “add over all possible values.”

μ = E[X] = Σ x P(X = x)
Why the mean alone is not enough

Compare two games. Game A always gives 10 points. Game B gives 0 or 20 points, equally likely. Both have an expected value of 10, but Game A is completely predictable while Game B varies from play to play.

To describe this difference, we need a measure of spread around the mean.

Variance: the average squared distance from the mean

Start with Game B, whose mean is μ = 10. Subtract the mean from each possible result. This difference is called a deviation:

  • For 0 points: 0 − 10 = −10 points.
  • For 20 points: 20 − 10 = +10 points.

Averaging these signed deviations gives zero because they cancel. That would hide the spread. Instead, we square each deviation, making both contributions positive, and then average them using their probabilities. Squaring also gives greater weight to larger distances.

Variance = (0 − 10)2 × (1/2) + (20 − 10)2 × (1/2)
= 100 × (1/2) + 100 × (1/2)
= 100 points2

We write the variance of X as Var(X). Since E[ ] means “take the probability-weighted average,” the following formula says exactly what we just calculated:

Var(X) = E[(X − μ)2]
= Σ (x − μ)2 P(X = x)

Game A has variance 0: every result equals its mean, so every deviation is zero. Variance cannot be negative.

Standard deviation: spread in the original units

Squaring the distances made variance's units points squared. To express spread in points again, take the square root. This gives the standard deviation, written σ (the Greek letter “sigma”).

σ = √Var(X)
For Game B: σ = √100 = 10 points

The mean describes centre; standard deviation describes spread around that centre. A smaller standard deviation indicates less overall spread. A larger one indicates greater overall spread. Compare these values using the same units.

Standard deviation is the square root of the average squared distance. It is not generally the average absolute distance, and it does not guarantee that every result lies within one standard deviation of the mean. The distribution's shape still matters.

Another way to calculate variance

You may also see the formula below. E[X²] means “square each possible value, then take the probability-weighted average.” (E[X])² means “find the mean first, then square it.” These operations usually give different results.

Var(X) = E[X2] − (E[X])2

For Game B, E[X²] = 0² × 1/2 + 20² × 1/2 = 200. The squared mean is 10² = 100. Therefore Var(X) = 200 − 100 = 100, matching the calculation using deviations.

This identity follows by expanding (X − μ)² and averaging, using E[X] = μ. It is a calculation shortcut for the same measure of spread.

Mean, median and mode describe different features

The mean averages the values. The median describes the middle of the ordered data, and the mode is the most frequent value. For the data 1, 1, 1, 9, the mean is 3, the median is 1, and the mode is 1. The large value pulls the mean upward.

For a probability distribution, a median has at least half the probability at or below it and at least half at or above it. A discrete mode has the highest probability; for a continuous density, a mode occurs where the density is highest. These quantities need not equal the expected value.

6. A percentile asks the question in reverse

A CDF asks, “How much probability is below x?” A q-quantile asks, “How far right must I go to accumulate at least q?” For 0 < q < 1, use the smallest x for which F(x) ≥ q. The 95th percentile uses q = 0.95.

In the head-count example, F(1) = 0.75 and F(2) = 1, so its 95th percentile is 2; there is no value with F(x) = 0.95. For a Normal distribution, the smooth, strictly increasing CDF allows equality at the chosen percentile.

7. How to use the graphs

The upper graph shows PMF heights or a PDF curve. The lower graph shows cumulative probability. For Binomial and Poisson, the selected count is marked on both graphs. For the Normal model, the shaded PDF area is the probability at or below your chosen x, matching the CDF value.

BERNOULLI DISTRIBUTION

Go to the graph & controls ↓

Inspect one component and record whether it is defective. Let X = 1 for a defect and X = 0 otherwise. This 0-or-1 variable has a Bernoulli distribution. It records one event happening or not happening.

Choose what “success” means

We call X = 1 a success simply because it is the event we are counting. A defect can therefore be a “success” in the mathematical notation. The underlying experiment can have many outcomes: a die roll becomes Bernoulli if you record 1 for a six and 0 for any other face.

The parameter p is P(X = 1), with 0 ≤ p ≤ 1. “Parameter” means a fixed number that specifies which member of a family of models you are using. Here one number determines everything:

P(X = 0) = 1−p   and   P(X = 1) = p

A 20% defect probability

Set p = 0.20. The PMF puts 0.80 at zero and 0.20 at one. For one component you observe either 0 or 1, never 0.20. Over many comparable trials, the mean number of defects per single trial tends toward 0.20.

Derive the mean and variance

E[X] = 0×(1−p) + 1×p = p. Because 0² = 0 and 1² = 1, X² = X, so E[X²] = p. Therefore:

E[X] = p

At p = 0.20, the variance is 0.16 and SD is 0.40. At p = 0.5, the variance is largest, 0.25. At p = 0 or 1 the recorded value is certain, so variance is zero.

From one trial to many

A Bernoulli variable describes one trial; it does not itself require a relationship between multiple trials. To count successes across n trials with a Binomial model, we additionally need independence and the same p in every trial.

BINOMIAL DISTRIBUTION

Go to the graph & controls ↓

Flip a coin four times and count the heads. Now the number of trials is fixed; the count of successes varies. Let X be the number of successes in n trials. Under the conditions below, X follows a Binomial distribution, written X ∼ Binomial(n,p). The symbol ∼ means “has this distribution.”

Four conditions define the model

There are a fixed integer n ≥ 0 trials; each trial is classified success or failure; trials are independent; and the success probability p is the same on every trial. Then X can equal 0,1,…,n. The calculator below uses n from 0 to 500.

Sampling without replacement from a small finite collection usually violates independence. For example, removing a defective item changes the fraction defective left. A Hypergeometric model handles that situation; see the model guide later in this chapter.

Build the formula from one sequence

For exactly k successes, one particular sequence has probability pk(1−p)n−k: multiply p once per success and 1−p once per failure. But the successes can occupy C(n,k) different sets of positions. Add those equally probable, mutually exclusive sequences.

P(X = k) = C(n,k)pk(1−p)n−k
C(n,k) = n! / [k!(n−k)!],   k = 0,…,n

Four independent trials, p = 0.25

For exactly two successes, there are C(4,2) = 6 patterns: SSFF, SFSF, SFFS, FSSF, FSFS, FFSS. Each has probability 0.25²×0.75² = 0.03515625.

Thus P(X = 2) = 6×0.03515625 = 0.2109375. For at most two, include zero and one: P(X ≤ 2) = 0.31640625 + 0.421875 + 0.2109375 = 0.94921875.

Watch the inequality

P(X ≤ k) = F(k)
P(X ≥ k) = 1−F(k−1)
P(X > k) = 1−F(k)

For the same example, P(X ≥ 2) = 1−F(1) = 0.26171875, whereas P(X > 2) = 1−F(2) = 0.05078125. “At least two” includes exactly two.

Centre and spread come from adding trials

Write X as the sum of n Bernoulli indicators. Each has mean p, and means add, giving np. Because the trials are independent, their variances also add, giving np(1−p).

E[X] = np;   Var(X) = np(1−p);   SD(X) = √[np(1−p)]

With n = 4 and p = 0.25, mean = 1 and variance = 0.75. The mean need not be the only possible result. At p = 0.5 the PMF is symmetric; small p puts most mass near zero. At p = 0 all mass is at zero; at p = 1 it is at n. If n = 0, the count is always zero.

POISSON DISTRIBUTION

Go to the graph & controls ↓

Count arrivals during a fixed interval, such as vehicles reaching a quiet checkpoint in ten minutes. There is no fixed number of Bernoulli trials in this description. We specify an expected count for the interval and model how the actual count varies.

A rate and an expected count are not the same number

Let r be the average rate in events per unit time and t the interval length in matching time units. The Poisson parameter is λ (“lambda”) = rt, the expected number of events in that interval.

Choose the interval before calculating

At a rate r = 3 arrivals per hour, a half-hour interval has λ = 3×0.5 = 1.5 arrivals. Enter 1.5 into the calculator, not 3. Changing the interval changes λ; it does not change the assumed underlying rate.

When is the Poisson arrival model reasonable?

For a homogeneous Poisson process, counts in non-overlapping intervals are independent, the rate is constant, and the chance of two or more arrivals in a sufficiently short interval is negligible relative to its length. Arrivals should not systematically travel in groups or trigger one another.

These are modeling assumptions. Rush-hour traffic or clustered failures may violate them. Merely knowing an average count is not enough to establish a Poisson model.

Probability of a count

Let X be the count. Its possible values are 0,1,2,… with no finite upper limit. For λ > 0:

P(X = k) = e−λ λk / k!,   k = 0,1,2,…

Here e ≈ 2.71828 is the base of natural logarithms; k! means k factorial, with 0! = 1. If λ = 0, no events occur under the model: P(X = 0) = 1.

Half an hour with λ = 1.5

P(X = 0) = e−1.5 ≈ 0.223130. P(X = 2) = e−1.5×1.5²/2 ≈ 0.251021.

P(X ≥ 1) = 1−P(X = 0) ≈ 0.776870. P(X ≥ 2) = 1−P(X = 0)−P(X = 1) ≈ 0.442175. An expected count of 1.5 is an average, not a possible integer count.

Mean, spread and a useful connection

E[X] = λ;   Var(X) = λ;   SD(X) = √λ

The equality of mean and variance is a feature of this model. As λ increases, the count grows and the absolute spread increases, while SD/mean = 1/√λ decreases for λ > 0.

Poisson also approximates Binomial counts in a rare-event setting: as n grows and p shrinks with np = λ held fixed, the Binomial probabilities approach Poisson probabilities. There is no single cutoff that guarantees a good approximation for every tail question.

NORMAL DISTRIBUTION

Go to the graph & controls ↓

Suppose a manufacturing process produces lengths scattered around 100 mm, with a typical spread of 2 mm. If a symmetric bell-shaped model is appropriate, write X ∼ N(100, 2²). X is the length, the mean μ (“mu”) is 100 mm, and the standard deviation σ (“sigma”) is 2 mm.

Two parameters control the curve

In the notation N(μ,σ²), the second parameter is the variance. The input below asks for σ itself, not σ². Changing μ moves the curve horizontally. Increasing σ spreads the probability over a wider range, making the peak lower because total area must stay 1.

f(x) = [1 / (σ√(2π))] exp[−½((x−μ)/σ)²],   σ > 0

exp(u) means e raised to the power u. You do not need to memorize the density formula to read it: deviations x−μ are scaled by σ, squared to make both sides symmetric, and converted to a curve whose area is 1.

The height at x is not a probability

The PDF value f(x) has units of 1/mm in the length example. P(X = 102) = 0 in the continuous model, but an interval near 102 can have positive probability. A tall, narrow curve may have density above 1 without violating any probability rule.

F(x) = P(X ≤ x)
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = F(b)−F(a)
P(X > x) = 1−F(x)

The CDF accumulates PDF area from the far left. Below, the shaded area in the upper graph and the selected value in the lower graph represent the same probability. The Normal curve extends beyond the graph in both directions.

A z-score expresses distance in standard deviations

z = (x−μ)/σ;   Z = (X−μ)/σ ∼ N(0,1)

For μ = 100, σ = 2 and x = 102, z = 1: this length is one standard deviation above the mean. Negative z is below the mean; z = 0 is at the mean. Standardizing puts any Normal model onto the same reference scale.

Lengths between 98 and 102 mm

The endpoints have z = −1 and z = 1. F(102) ≈ 0.841345 and F(98) ≈ 0.158655, so P(98 ≤ X ≤ 102) ≈ 0.682689. The chance above 102 is approximately 0.158655.

At x = μ, exactly half the probability lies below and half above. Mean, median and mode all equal μ for a Normal distribution.

The 68–95–99.7 rule and its limits

For a Normal model, approximately 68.27% lies within μ±σ, 95.45% within μ±2σ and 99.73% within μ±3σ. These percentages are not universal rules for every distribution.

A Normal model allows negative and positive values without bound. It may be poor for strongly skewed data, small counts, or measurements close to a hard boundary such as zero. Many small influences can support a Normal approximation under suitable conditions, but that does not make every measurement Normal.

CENTRAL LIMIT THEOREM

The raw results of a fair die are the six equally likely integers 1–6. They do not form a bell curve. Now roll the die n times and calculate one average. Repeat that entire experiment many times, always using fresh rolls. The collection of averages has a new distribution: a sampling distribution.

Keep two counts separate

Sample size n is the number of observations inside one average. Number of repeated samples is how many averages you calculate to see their distribution. Increasing the number of repeated samples reveals the existing distribution more clearly. Increasing n changes the distribution of the averages.

The classical Central Limit Theorem

Assume observations X₁,…,Xₙ are independent and identically distributed (the same probability model), with finite mean μ and finite, positive variance σ². Their random sample mean is X̄ = (X₁+⋯+Xₙ)/n. The standardized mean approaches a standard Normal distribution as n grows:

(X̄−μ) / (σ/√n) approaches N(0,1)
For sufficiently large n: X̄ is approximately N(μ,σ²/n)

The raw data do not become Normal. The result concerns averages or sums. If the original observations are themselves Normal, the sample mean is exactly Normal for every n, under independence.

Spread of individual values versus spread of averages

The individual-value SD is σ. The SD of the sample mean is σ/√n, called its standard error. Under the assumptions above, the following mean and variance formulas are exact even when the Normal approximation is imperfect:

E[X̄] = μ;   Var(X̄) = σ²/n;   SD(X̄) = σ/√n

Averages of 36 fair-die rolls

For one roll, μ = 3.5 and variance = 35/12 ≈ 2.9167, so σ ≈ 1.7078. For an average of 36 independent rolls, the mean is still 3.5 but standard error is 1.7078/6 ≈ 0.2846.

The Normal approximation puts about 95% of such averages within 1.96 standard errors of 3.5: roughly 2.942 to 4.058. These are bounds for repeated sample averages, not for individual die faces.

There is no universal “n = 30” guarantee

Required sample size depends on skewness, tail behaviour and the accuracy needed. Strong dependence or infinite variance can invalidate this classical theorem. Very rare events and extreme tails often need extra care.

LLN versus CLT

The LLN says a sample average concentrates near the population mean. The CLT describes the approximate shape and scale of its remaining fluctuations. To halve the standard error, multiply n by four. These ideas lead into estimation and confidence intervals; they do not establish that any one realised interval must contain the true mean.

COUNTING POSSIBILITIES

Suppose you choose a shirt from 3 options and trousers from 2 options. Each shirt pairs with either pair of trousers, making 3 × 2 = 6 outfits. This is the multiplication principle for counting. It counts possibilities; it is not an assumption about probabilistic independence.

Does order matter?

Choose two people from Ada, Ben and Cora. For a chair and a secretary, Ada–Ben and Ben–Ada are different assignments: order matters. For a two-person team they are the same team: order does not matter.

Ordered rolesUnordered teams
AB, AC, BA, BC, CA, CBAB, AC, BC
3 × 2 = 6 assignments6 / 2 = 3 teams

Factorials count arrangements

n! (“n factorial”) means n × (n−1) × ⋯ × 1. For example, 4! = 24. We define 0! = 1; there is one way to arrange no items. For n distinct items and 0 ≤ r ≤ n:

Ordered selections without replacement: n! / (n−r)!
Unordered selections without replacement: C(n,r) = n! / [r!(n−r)!]

The combination formula divides by r! because every unordered group appears in r! different orders. C(n,r), also written “n choose r,” counts groups; it is not itself a probability.

Can an item appear again?

A three-digit code using digits 0–9 has 10³ = 1,000 possibilities if repetition and a leading zero are allowed. If no digit can repeat, there are 10 × 9 × 8 = 720. Restrictions change the count.

From counting to probability

Two independent fair dice have 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely ordered outcomes. A total of 7 occurs as (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1): six outcomes. Therefore P(sum = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6.

The possible sums 2 through 12 are not equally likely. A sum of 2 comes only from (1,1). Counting “one favourable sum out of eleven sums” would give the wrong answer.

CHOOSING A MODEL

Choose a model by describing what is being recorded and how the experiment works. A bell-shaped picture or a familiar formula alone is not enough.

Geometric: how many tries until the first success?

Let X count trials including the first success. For independent trials with success probability 0 < p ≤ 1, X can be 1,2,… . For the first success to occur at k, the first k−1 trials must fail and trial k must succeed.

P(X = k) = (1−p)k−1p
P(X ≤ k) = 1−(1−p)k
E[X] = 1/p;   Var(X) = (1−p)/p²

For p = 0.2, the chance of first succeeding on trial 3 is 0.8²×0.2 = 0.128; the chance of success within 3 trials is 1−0.8³ = 0.488. The mean wait is 5 trials. Some books count failures before success instead, with support 0,1,…; always check the convention.

Uniform: use interval length

If X is uniform on [a,b], with b > a, its PDF equals 1/(b−a) on that interval and zero outside. A subinterval’s probability is its length divided by b−a.

E[X] = (a+b)/2;   Var(X) = (b−a)²/12

For an assumed uniform wait from 0 to 10 minutes, the chance of 2–5 minutes is 3/10; the mean is 5 minutes and SD is 10/√12 ≈ 2.887 minutes. Lack of information alone does not prove that a real waiting time is uniform.

Exponential: waiting time in a Poisson process

A Poisson count asks how many arrivals occur in a fixed time. An Exponential wait asks how long until the next one. Let r > 0 be the constant rate in events per unit time, and T the waiting time.

For t ≥ 0: P(T > t) = e−rt;   F(t) = 1−e−rt
f(t) = r e−rt;   E[T] = 1/r;   Var(T) = 1/r²

At 3 arrivals per hour, the mean wait is 1/3 hour = 20 minutes. The chance of waiting longer than half an hour is e−1.5 ≈ 0.223130. This equals the probability of zero Poisson arrivals in that half hour.

The Geometric and Exponential distributions are memoryless: conditional on no success or arrival yet, the remaining wait has the same distribution as a new wait. They suit constant-chance models, not all real waiting or ageing processes.

Sampling without replacement: Hypergeometric

A collection of N items contains K successes. Choose n items uniformly without replacement, and count successes X. The changing composition creates dependence.

P(X = k) = C(K,k) C(N−K,n−k) / C(N,n)

From 10 components with 3 defects, choose 2. The probability that both are defective is (3/10)×(2/9) = 1/15 ≈ 0.066667. A Binomial calculation (0.3)² = 0.09 would be wrong for this sampling scheme. Valid k must satisfy 0 ≤ k ≤ K and 0 ≤ n−k ≤ N−K.

Questions to ask before calculating

What exactly does X record? What values can it take? Is the number of trials fixed, or are we waiting for an event? Are trials independent? Does the probability or rate change? Do the units match? A carefully defined experiment is more useful than a memorized formula used in the wrong setting.
